COLORADO'S FIRST DEEPFAKE DISCLOSURE CASE: WHAT ORGANIZATIONS SHOULD TAKE FROM IT

October 12, 2026  |  Kronus Intelligence Group

A Colorado complaint over an AI-generated video of a state senator is testing a deepfake disclosure law. The case is unresolved, but the practical lessons are already clear.

BLUF: Colorado's Secretary of State has filed what appears to be the first complaint under the state's deepfake disclosure law, over an AI-generated video of a sitting state senator. The case is pending and nothing has been decided. It matters to organizations because it shows how a synthetic video of a named person can become a legal dispute, a news story and a reputational event at once, and because the rules differ by state. Confidence in the facts below is high for the filings and moderate for how the case will end.

What Is Alleged

The account below follows the state's complaint, the Colorado Secretary of State's hearing docket, and reporting by CBS Colorado and Colorado Newsline.

  • The video. The Independence Institute, a Colorado think tank, distributed an AI-generated video urging voters to oppose Proposition NN, a measure on the November 3, 2026 ballot. The complaint says the video depicts state Sen. Cathy Kipp saying or doing things she did not say or do.
  • The law. Colorado's HB 24-1147, codified at Section 1-46-103, prohibits distributing election-related material with AI-generated deepfakes of candidates or officeholders without a clear and conspicuous disclaimer.
  • The disclosure. Reporting says the video carried a disclaimer reading, in effect, "AI generated content because who gets a human cashier these days?" The complaint alleges that this was smaller than other text and did not use the required wording.
  • The process. Sen. Kipp first filed a complaint in August. The Elections Division then filed its own complaint in September. Reporting describes it as potentially the first under the law.

What Is Contested

The Independence Institute's position, as reported, is that a law restricting speech about elected officials is a First Amendment violation. A motion to dismiss appears on the state's hearing docket dated October 8. We have not seen a ruling, and none should be assumed.

What we cannot say: whether the disclaimer was legally sufficient, whether the law will survive challenge, or what penalty, if any, applies. Those are for the hearing officer and courts.

Why It Matters Beyond Politics

This is an election case, but the pattern is general. A realistic video of a named person circulates. The person objects. The question of what was disclosed, and how visibly, becomes the story. Executives, board members and public-facing principals face the same exposure outside elections, usually without a statute built for it.

Two points stand out. First, a joke or a label does not settle the matter. A visible notice that content is synthetic may satisfy a reader's curiosity, yet still fall short of a legal standard. Second, laws are patchwork. Requirements differ by state and by context, so a communications team cannot assume one rule applies everywhere. Our earlier briefing on the Fideuram deepfake voice fraud case covers the financial side of the same technology.

What to Do Now

  1. Keep verified reference material for your principals. Authentic video, audio and statements, dated and stored, make it faster to show what is real.
  2. Monitor for synthetic media of named people. Include video platforms and small outlets, not only your own channels. See our guide to narrative threat monitoring.
  3. Decide in advance who authorizes a public denial. The hours after a fake appears are not the time to find out who can speak.
  4. Know the platform and jurisdiction rules. Most platforms have synthetic media policies, and several states have disclosure laws. Have counsel map the ones that touch your principals.
  5. If you publish synthetic content yourself, disclose it clearly. Use plain wording, legible size and placement, and check the statute where you operate. Satire does not automatically exempt a piece.
  6. Preserve evidence. Capture the content, the URL, time and spread before it is removed.

Confidence and Caveats

We assess with high confidence that the complaint and motion exist as described, since they appear on the state's public docket. We assess with low confidence on the outcome, because the case is early and legal arguments remain open. We have not independently reviewed the video. This note will be updated if a ruling is issued.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Colorado deepfake disclosure case? The Colorado Secretary of State's Elections Division filed a complaint against the Independence Institute over an AI-generated video that depicted state Sen. Cathy Kipp and opposed Proposition NN. The complaint alleges the video lacked the clear and conspicuous disclaimer Colorado law requires. The matter is pending.

What does Colorado's deepfake law require? Under HB 24-1147, election-related communications that contain AI-generated depictions of candidates or officeholders saying or doing things they did not must carry a clear and conspicuous disclaimer. The state alleges the disclaimer used here was smaller than other text and did not use the statutory wording.

Has anyone been found in violation? No. As of October 12, 2026 the case was pending. The Independence Institute disputes the claim, arguing among other things that the law violates the First Amendment, and a motion to dismiss was filed on October 8.

What should organizations do about deepfakes of their leaders? Keep verified reference material for principals, set up monitoring for synthetic media, agree in advance who can authorize a public denial, and document takedown and disclosure rules for each platform and jurisdiction.
